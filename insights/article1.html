The River That Charges Two Prices

One acre-foot, two prices — and a spread that is the business of valuing Western water.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

The hearing room in Phoenix was not dramatic. No raised voices. A utility manager read a number off a spreadsheet, a farmer's representative read another, and for a moment the two figures sat side by side in the air like an accusation. One acre-foot of Colorado River water — 326,000 gallons, enough for two or three households for a year — cost one party about $30. It cost the other about $512.

That gap is the central fact of the modern Western water market. A December 2025 report from the University of California, Los Angeles Institute of the Environment and Sustainability and the Natural Resources Defense Council put it in hard numbers. Researchers examined 217 water transactions across the Lower Basin states of Arizona, California, and Nevada. Primarily agricultural districts paid a weighted average of $30.32 per acre-foot. Municipal districts paid $512.01. Nearly a quarter of the water diverted to agricultural irrigation districts in those three states was obtained for zero dollars from the federal Bureau of Reclamation.

Noah Garrison, a water researcher at UCLA and a co-author of the report, put the point bluntly in coverage of the findings: the system is effectively giving away millions of acre-feet per year, free of charge or nearly so, from a river system that is in crisis. The Colorado River serves 40 million people and 5.5 million acres of agricultural land across the Western United States and northwestern Mexico. A river under that much demand cannot also be priced as though supply were infinite. Yet, in large stretches of its plumbing, it still is.

The reason is history, not conspiracy. The river's allocations were drawn up in an era when the West's problem looked like underdevelopment, not scarcity. Federal projects built the dams and canals, and the contracts that followed charged users largely for the cost of storing, moving, and treating water — not for the water itself. Senior agricultural districts, above all California's Imperial Irrigation District, the single largest holder of Colorado River water rights, pay the Bureau of Reclamation nothing at all for the raw supply under their contracts. The price signal, where one exists at all, reflects concrete and pumps, not scarcity.

Then the federal government started paying to leave water in the river, and the market got a glimpse of a second price. In 2023, Imperial Irrigation District agreed to a one-year conservation contract at about $776 per acre-foot, after asking for as much as $840. Three-year deals with about two dozen users — including Phoenix, Tucson, and Scottsdale — were struck at $400 per acre-foot. Lower Basin conservation contracts totaled nearly $664 million. The single largest deal, a four-year pact to leave 351,000 acre-feet in Lake Mead, was signed with the Metropolitan Water District of Southern California and the Palo Verde Irrigation District. Arizona's largest deal, with the Gila River Indian Community, covered 341,000 acre-feet.

Read those numbers together and a pattern forms. The same water that costs an agricultural district $30 on a contract can command $400 or $777 when a city or the federal government needs it left in a reservoir. That spread is not a rounding error. It is the distance between an administered price set decades ago and a market price set by present scarcity. For a brokerage or valuation firm, the spread is the business: every transfer, lease, or fallowing agreement is, at bottom, an attempt to move water from the first price toward the second without breaking the legal and political frame that holds both in place.

The counterargument deserves a fair hearing, because it is not frivolous. Agricultural districts argue, with some justice, that their low contract prices were the bargain struck for building the system in the first place — that farmers took the risk, settled the desert, and fed cities that now want the water back at a discount to its replacement cost. They note, correctly, that a farm's water use is not waste by definition: it becomes food, jobs, and export revenue. They also note that municipal prices bundle treatment, distribution, and system reliability that farm deliveries do not include, so $30 and $512 are not quite like-for-like. All true. None of it erases the core distortion: when one class of user pays nothing for the resource itself, there is no incentive to conserve at the margin, and no honest signal about where the next acre-foot creates the most value.

Original analysis points in one direction. Expect the two-price system to narrow, but not by the administered price rising smoothly to meet the market price. It will narrow deal by deal — compensated fallowing, system conservation agreements, tribal settlements, and city-to-farm leases — each priced off the replacement cost of the next alternative. That replacement cost is steep. Recycled water starts near $180 per acre-foot and rises with treatment. Demand management and groundwater recharge cluster near a $390 median. Coastal desalination runs $800 to $1,400 per acre-foot; inland Colorado River Basin desalination exceeds $3,000, with five to ten years of permitting and construction before first water. Against that ladder, a $400 conservation payment looks less like a subsidy and more like a bargain. A valuation that ignores the ladder will underprice senior, reliable rights and overprice junior rights that vanish first in a shortage.

The implication for buyers is practical, not philosophical. Price the right, not the average. Seniority, source, delivery infrastructure, and the legal path to transfer matter more than the basin average, because the basin average blends two different markets. The $30 figure tells you what the old system charges. The $512 figure tells you what a city will pay to keep taps running. The $400 and $777 figures tell you what the federal government will pay to keep a reservoir from falling further. A firm that can explain, parcel by parcel, which of those prices applies to a given right — and what could move it from one column to the other — owns the most valuable position in the market: the position of the trusted measurer.
