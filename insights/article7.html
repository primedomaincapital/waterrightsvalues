Groundwater vs. Surface Water: Which Right Holds Its Value When the River Runs Low

A surface right is a claim on a river that may not flow. A groundwater right is a claim on an aquifer the state is now metering. When the river runs low, the two rights move in opposite directions — and the gap is the trade.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The farmer in the Tulare Lake basin owns two water rights. The first is a surface right on a river that has run low in three of the last five years, senior enough that it still delivers in most seasons, junior enough that a dry winter can cut it to nothing. The second is a groundwater right under his own land, pumped from wells he drilled twenty years ago, reliable in every drought he has lived through. He wants to sell one and keep the other. The broker tells him the surface right is worth more, because surface water is the water everyone can see. The farmer is not sure. He has watched the river shrink and the wells deepen, and he has read the letters from the state about the basin his wells sit in. He is right to hesitate. When the river runs low, the two rights do not move together. They move in opposite directions, and the gap between them is the trade.

The surface right is the older asset, and its value is set by the oldest rule in western water law: prior appropriation, first in time, first in right. A senior surface right is a claim on the river that is honored before every junior claim, and in a normal year that seniority is worth a great deal. The price record shows how much. The Nasdaq Veles California Water Index, which tracks the price of surface water in the state’s five most actively traded regions, stood near $225 an acre-foot in early 2020, rose to a peak of $1,282 in September 2022, during the driest stretch of the current drought cycle, and fell to $156 by July 2023, when the winter rains returned. On October 28, 2026, it stood at $522.38. About 1.5 million acre-feet change hands in California every year, according to the Public Policy Institute of California, and most of that volume is surface water moving between agriculture, cities, and the environment. The surface right is a liquid asset with a published price, and that liquidity is a large part of its value.

The surface right’s weakness is the same as its strength: it is a claim on a river, and rivers do not always flow. A temporary transfer in California can clear in 60 days, as the last article in this series showed, but a transfer is only useful if there is water to transfer. In a dry year, the State Water Resources Control Board curtails junior diverters, and the curtailment list is the document that decides who gets water and who does not. A senior right survives curtailment. A junior right does not. The value of a surface right is therefore not a single number. It is a distribution, wide in a wet year and narrow in a dry one, and the buyer who pays the wet-year price for a junior right has bought a dry-year disappointment.

The groundwater right is the mirror image. It is a claim on an aquifer, not a river, and its value has always been its reliability. A well does not depend on the winter’s snowpack. It depends on the aquifer, and the aquifer has been the drought insurance of California agriculture for a century. That insurance is now being repriced by the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, the 2014 law that requires the state’s overdrafted basins to reach sustainability by 2040 for critically overdrafted basins and 2042 for the rest. The law put the aquifers under management for the first time, and management means limits: pumping allocations, metering, and fees, set by local Groundwater Sustainability Agencies and enforced, where the local agencies fail, by the State Water Resources Control Board.

The enforcement is no longer theoretical. The Tulare Lake subbasin, the farmer’s basin, has been on probationary status since April 16, 2024, the first basin in the state to be placed under state intervention. Probation carries fees of $300 per well per year plus $20 per acre-foot pumped, and it carries reporting requirements that turn a private well into a metered, reported, and charged asset. The Paso Robles Area Groundwater Authority adopted a pumping fee of $22.90 per acre-foot on May 27, 2026, one of the first such fees in the state. The fees are small beside the value of the water. The direction is not. Groundwater is moving from an unmetered common resource to a metered, allocated, and charged one, and the rights that were once the most reliable in the system are now the most regulated.

The market is beginning to price the difference. A surface right in a basin with a reliable river and a senior priority is the most valuable water asset in the West, because it is liquid, it is priced, and it is transferable. A groundwater right in a basin under probation is discounted, because the pumping allocation may shrink, the fees will rise, and the state may intervene further. A groundwater right in a basin that has already achieved sustainability, with a functioning local agency and a stable allocation, is the opposite: it is the most drought-proof asset in the system, and its value rises precisely when the surface rights fall, because it is the water that is still there when the river is not.

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because it is the argument the groundwater owners make, and it is not wrong. Groundwater is the only water that is available in a drought, and a drought is when water is worth the most. The surface right’s price collapses when the river is full and everyone has water, and it spikes when the river is empty and nobody does, which means the surface right is at its most valuable exactly when it is least likely to deliver. The groundwater right delivers in both seasons. The regulation that discounts it is also the regulation that will eventually stabilize it, because a basin that reaches sustainability has a pumping allocation that can be relied on, and a reliable allocation is an asset. The farmer who sells his groundwater right to keep his surface right may be selling the insurance and keeping the exposure.

The counterargument fails on the timeline, not the logic. Sustainability is a 2040 deadline, and the years between now and then are the years in which the allocations are set, the fees are imposed, and the basins that fail are put on probation. A groundwater right bought today is a bet on a regulatory process that has not finished, in a basin whose final allocation is not yet known. The surface right, by contrast, is a bet on a river whose hydrology is known, even if the water is not always there. The two rights are not substitutes. They are complements, and the portfolio that holds both is worth more than the sum of its parts, because the surface right pays in wet years and the groundwater right pays in dry ones.

The practical version is a checklist, not a verdict. Before buying a surface right, check the priority date, the curtailment history, and the transfer path, because the price is only as good as the delivery. Before buying a groundwater right, check the basin’s status under the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, the local agency’s allocation, and the fees already in place, because the right is only as good as the pumping it allows. Before selling either, ask which one the buyer’s grandchildren will want in a dry year, and remember that the river runs low on a schedule nobody controls, while the aquifer runs low on a schedule the state is now writing. The right that holds its value is the one whose risks the buyer has already priced, and the price that matters is not the one on the index. It is the one on the well, in the year the river does not come.
