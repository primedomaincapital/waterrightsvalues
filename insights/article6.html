How Long a Water Rights Transfer Takes — and Where It Stalls

A temporary transfer in California can clear in 60 days. A permanent change of use in Colorado can take years. The clock, not the price, kills most water deals.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The petition was filed in January, for water the buyer needed in July. That is the ordinary shape of a western water transfer: a city, a district, or a grower agrees a price in the winter and then waits to find out whether the state will let the water move before the season it was bought for. In California in 2026, the answer depends on which clock the transfer runs on. The State Water Resources Control Board's own guidance draws the line sharply. A temporary transfer — water moved for a year or less — carries a statutory decision deadline of 35 days from public notice or the start of the Board's investigation, whichever is later, and the Board's stated goal is action within 60 days. A long-term transfer, anything over a year, must clear California Environmental Quality Act review first, and the Board says processing can extend from up to a year to several years. The same buyer, the same water, the same river — and the difference between a decision in two months and a decision in three years is a single word in the petition.

California's fast clock is real, and the state has spent 2026 making it faster. In January 2026 the Board announced a streamlined noticing process for temporary transfers that cuts the maximum processing time from 65 days to 55 days, by speeding up the public notice step. The catch is in the fine print: to use it, a transferor had to notify the Board by January 31, 2026 of water rights it intended to transfer during the year, and the notice is an expression of intent, not a commitment. The Board must still give public notice within ten days of receiving a petition, and the Water Code still allows extensions to the deadlines. The slower clocks run alongside. A minor change to a water right can take about three months, according to the Board's own FAQ; a major or controversial change takes longer, and the most controversial can take as long as 10 years to process. A new appropriation — a fresh right rather than a transfer of an existing one — is slower still: the Board estimates five to seven years for a regular-priority project from application to decision, and its FAQ puts the usual range at a minimum of one year and more likely three to four. Even the paperwork step has a clock: a change of ownership recorded in the Board's CalWATRS system takes two to six weeks for staff review, depending on complexity. The system is not slow in one place. It is a set of different speeds, and the speed depends on what is being asked.

Colorado runs on a different machine entirely, and it is the slowest of the three states in this comparison by design. A change of water right in Colorado is not an administrative approval. It is a case in water court, filed with the water clerk in one of seven divisions, published in a monthly resume, and decided by a referee or a water judge. The statute sets the first hard deadline: a statement of opposition must be filed by the last day of the second month following the month in which the application is filed, under Colorado Revised Statutes 37-92-302. If the change needs a well, no hearing on the merits can happen until the State Engineer files a consultation report, due within four months of filing. After that, there is no statutory clock. The case moves through engineering review, negotiation with opposers, and, if it cannot settle, trial. The monthly resume system means every filing is public and every holder of a nearby right gets a chance to object, and the ten-year rule hangs over the whole market: ten years of non-use can put a right at risk of abandonment, so a buyer is racing a different clock as well — the seller's own history of use. The price of that process is certainty: a decreed change of use in Colorado is a court order, and it sticks in a way an administrative approval does not. The cost is time measured in years, not months.

New Mexico sits between the two, and its procedure shows where transfers actually stall. An application to the Office of the State Engineer goes through notice published once a week for three consecutive weeks in a newspaper of general circulation in the county where the right sits. Under the water-use leasing statute, a protest must be filed within ten days after the last publication, and if a protest is filed the State Engineer must hold a hearing, with notice to the parties. If no protest is filed, the State Engineer may grant the application without a hearing. The initial review of an application runs within sixty days of receipt under the state's storage and recovery statute, and an applicant who disagrees with the State Engineer's action has thirty days to request a hearing. On paper, an unprotested transfer in New Mexico can move in a few months. In practice, the stall is the protest. A single neighboring holder who can show the change would impair an existing right, run contrary to conservation, or harm the public welfare can force a hearing, and the hearing record — hydrology, historic use, return flows — is where months become years. The stakes are visible in the state's own ledger: after Texas sued New Mexico in 2013 over Rio Grande deliveries, the settlement reached in 2026 requires New Mexico to curb groundwater pumping and buy water rights from farmers at a cost reported at more than $150 million. When a state is buying its way out of a lawsuit, every transfer in the basin is priced against a deadline the state did not choose.

The carrying cost of the wait is the part buyers budget last and feel first. A transfer that takes three years instead of three months costs the buyer three irrigation seasons, three years of lease payments to bridge the gap, and three years of legal and engineering fees with no water to show for them. The bridge price is visible in the lease market: in California's 2014 drought, spot water reached $2,200 an acre-foot, and a buyer waiting on a long-term approval while farming through a dry year pays that rate for every acre-foot it cannot yet move. The value at risk is visible in the permanent market: a Colorado-Big Thompson unit at $57,487 in 2025 delivers 0.80 acre-feet in 2026, so a buyer who closes on the unit but waits two years for a change decree has paid the full price for two years of water it cannot use. The delay also changes the asset. A right that cannot be moved to its new point of diversion is worth the price of its old use, not its new one, and a seller who watches a transfer stall for a second year has a strong incentive to walk away and sell to a buyer whose use needs no change at all. Time does not just add cost to a water deal. It reprices the deal, in the seller's favor, every season it runs.

The counterargument is that the delay is the protection, not the problem, and the states that move fastest have the thinnest safeguards. Colorado's water court is slow because a change of use can dry up a neighbor's ditch, strand a reservoir's return flows, or move a senior call onto a junior holder who never agreed to it — injuries that are real, measurable, and irreversible once the water moves. California's 35-day temporary clock works precisely because a temporary transfer is limited to a year and reversible by definition; the state can afford speed because the exposure is capped. New Mexico's protest hearing exists because the alternative — letting a transfer through on paper while a neighbor's well goes dry — is worse than a slow approval. The fastest transfer in the West is the one nobody objects to, and the reason nobody objects is usually that the applicant did the work before filing: historic-use records in order, return-flow analysis done, neighbours talked to before the notice is published. The system rewards preparation and punishes surprise. That is not a bug in the process. It is the process.

The practical read is to treat the timeline as part of the price, and to buy accordingly. In California, file a temporary transfer for the water needed this season and a long-term petition for the water needed next decade, and do not confuse the two clocks. In Colorado, budget years, not months, for a change of use; buy rights whose existing use already matches the intended use wherever possible; and treat the opposition deadline — the last day of the second month after filing — as the first real test of whether the deal will close. In New Mexico, price the protest risk before the purchase price: an unprotested transfer is a three-month transaction and a protested one is a multi-year hearing, and the difference is visible in the hydrology before the application is filed. And in all three states, put the bridge water in the budget — the lease, at whatever the dry-year rate is, that keeps the farm or the city running while the state takes its time. The water market does not reward the buyer who finds the cheapest acre-foot. It rewards the buyer who knows, to the month, when that acre-foot will actually arrive.
