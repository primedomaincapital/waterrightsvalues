Seniority Is the New Scarcity

Not all stored water is usable water. What survives a shortage is seniority, a flowing source, delivery, and a lawful transfer path.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

The boat ramp at Lake Powell ends in dust now, a long way from the water it was built to serve. That image has become shorthand for the Colorado River's troubles, but the more consequential story is not the photograph. It is the ledger underneath it: how much water is actually stored, how much of that storage can actually be used, and what a right to a reliable slice of it is worth when the river delivers less than the law promises.

Start with the inflow. The Colorado Basin River Forecast Center predicted in early June 2025 that unregulated snowmelt inflow to Lake Powell would end the year at 54% of the recent 30-year average. In the 21st century, only 2002, 2018, and 2021 had lower inflows to Powell. The 46 reservoirs tracked across the basin gained only 0.55 million acre-feet during the 2025 snowmelt season. Of that, only 0.28 million acre-feet accumulated in the 12 federal reservoirs, and only 0.11 million acre-feet accumulated in Powell and Mead combined. By July 1, 2025, all of that Powell-plus-Mead accumulation had been released downstream or evaporated.

Storage figures from June 30, 2025, make the thin margin concrete. Active storage in 42 reservoirs upstream from Lake Powell stood at 8.58 million acre-feet. Lake Mead held 8.05 million. Lake Powell held 7.88 million. Those are large numbers until they are set against demand that was planned for a river delivering considerably more, year after year, than it now does.

Jack Schmidt of the Center for Colorado River Studies at Utah State University, writing with Anne Castle, John Fleck, Eric Kuhn, Kathryn Sorensen, and Katherine Tara of the Colorado River Research Group, added a correction that every valuation should carry. Not all stored water is usable water. Hydroelectricity cannot be produced at Lake Powell below 3,490 feet, and 3,500 feet has been established as a minimum safe level for intake through the penstocks. The volume of active storage in Powell above dead pool but below 3,500 feet is 4.2 million acre-feet — water that is present but cannot safely be withdrawn through the penstocks, with continuous use of the river outlets considered unwise. At Hoover Dam, 4.5 million acre-feet of active storage sits below 1,000 feet, also not realistically accessible. Across the two largest reservoirs, 8.7 million acre-feet sits below the elevations required for safe and efficient operation. Of the 14.9 million acre-feet of active storage at Powell and Mead on November 15, 2025, only 42% — 6.2 million acre-feet — was realistically accessible.

That distinction reframes the market. A buyer who pays for a share of "storage" without asking about elevation thresholds, penstock constraints, and release rules is paying for water that may not be deliverable when it is needed most. A seller who can document a senior, high-priority right with a reliable delivery path is selling something else entirely: not a hope, but a call on water that survives a shortage declaration.

The price evidence for that premium is already visible in Colorado. A senior Colorado-Big Thompson Project water right — a federal trans-basin project serving the Northern Front Range — traded at roughly $85,000 per acre-foot in 2025, according to market reporting on alternative assets. Arizona water prices climbed 75% from 2022 to 2023 and remained near those highs. Those are permanent-right prices, not annual lease rates, and they should not be compared directly with the $30 or $512 annual figures from the Lower Basin. The comparison that matters is within each class: senior, reliable, transferable rights are pulling away from junior, interruptible rights, even in the same basin, because buyers have learned to pay for certainty rather than volume on paper.

The counterargument is that seniority can be overstated, and sometimes it is. A senior right on a tributary that runs dry still delivers nothing. A right that cannot be transferred — because of a "no injury" rule, an endangered-species constraint, or a change-of-use proceeding that takes years and trims the yield — may be worth far less than its priority date suggests. Political risk cuts the other way, too: tribes, environmental claimants, and Upper Basin states all hold claims that could reorder priorities in a post-2026 operating regime, and a court or a negotiated settlement can do what a drought cannot. Michael Burry, the investor known for his housing bet, made a related point in a 2010 interview: transporting water is impractical for political and physical reasons, so buying water rights outright did not make sense to him; growing food in water-rich areas and transporting the food was, in his view, the less contentious way to move embedded water. That skepticism is a useful check on any pitch that treats a water right like a stock certificate.

The check does not cancel the thesis. It refines it. Value concentrates not in "water rights" as a category, but in rights that pass four tests: seniority that survives a shortage, a source that still flows in a dry year, infrastructure that can actually deliver, and a lawful transfer path that has been walked before. Rights that pass all four will command a scarcity premium that widens as reservoirs thin. Rights that fail one test will trade at a discount that widens just as fast. The spread between those two groups — not the average price of water — is the signal a valuation firm must measure.

The implication for owners and acquirers is a discipline, not a slogan. Diligence must start with hydrology and law together: the priority date and the decree, the gauge record and the reservoir elevation, the delivery canal and the transfer precedent. Appraisals that cite a basin average, without adjusting for seniority, elevation, and transferability, will misprice both sides of a deal. The firms that build that diligence into a repeatable method — and that can show a client, in numbers, why one acre-foot is worth multiples of another acre-foot a valley away — will be the ones cities, districts, and investors call first when the next shortage declaration lands. In a market where the river delivers less than the paper promises, the measurer of real, deliverable water becomes the most trusted party in the room.
