What an Acre-Foot Costs, Basin by Basin

The same acre-foot of western water trades for $20, for $512, and for $85,000. The spread is not a pricing error. It is the market.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The auctioneer opened the bidding at $72,000 a unit and watched the room settle. It was February 2024 in northern Colorado, and the sellers were offering 90 shares of Colorado-Big Thompson Project water — the closest thing the American West has to a benchmark water asset. The first bid hit a high mark, prices steadied at around $46,000 a unit, and when the gavel stopped the sale had netted about $4.7 million for roughly 63 acre-feet of water. The buyers paid an average of $74,600 per acre-foot to own the water in perpetuity, according to Aspen Journalism's account of the auction. Two years earlier, the same water had traded at $101,000 an acre-foot. The river had not got wetter in the meantime. The reservoirs had not filled. The price fell while the scarcity deepened, and that contradiction is the first thing anyone pricing western water has to understand.

Start in the basin where the price is highest, because it is the one buyers cite most and understand least. Colorado-Big Thompson water traded at $85,000 per acre-foot in 2025, down from $101,000 in 2022, according to WestWater Research's market data reported by Water Education Colorado. The number needs a footnote that most citations skip: a C-BT unit is not an acre-foot. It is a 1/310,000 share of whatever volume Northern Water's board makes available in a given year, and Northern Water's own price series puts the unit at $57,487 in 2025, down from a $67,000 peak in 2023. A unit delivered 0.80 acre-feet in 2026 and averaged 0.762 acre-feet a year over 2006–2025, which is how the per-unit price converts to the per-acre-foot figure. The buyers are not paying for water in a river. They are paying for a firmed, stored, delivered supply on the Front Range, where population growth along the Interstate-25 corridor keeps adding houses faster than the water system adds supply. The same logic explains the City of Aurora's April 2024 purchase: $76.8 million for land in Otero County carrying about 7,500 acre-feet of water rights a year, roughly $10,240 per acre-foot, reported by Water Education Colorado. Aurora bought junior Arkansas Valley rights at a tenth of the C-BT price because the water is different — less reliable, further from the tap, and subject to a call it cannot control.

Cross the Continental Divide to the Lower Colorado, and the same acre-foot costs almost nothing — at wholesale, to the agencies that hold the oldest paper. A December 11, 2025 study from UCLA's Institute of the Environment and Sustainability and the Natural Resources Defense Council, built from a year of state and federal contracts, financial reports, and agency records, found that federally supplied Colorado River water is delivered at a weighted average wholesale price of about $0.12 per acre-foot. Agricultural districts in the study paid about $30 per acre-foot on average; municipal providers paid roughly $512. In California the gap widened to twenty times: cities paid about $722 per acre-foot against about $36 for farm suppliers, and some urban agencies paid more than $2,500 per acre-foot. Five major agricultural suppliers — including the Imperial, Coachella Valley, and Palo Verde irrigation districts — paid nothing to the federal government for nearly 4 million acre-feet of water. The Imperial Irrigation District charges its farmers $20 per acre-foot, a rate its water department manager, Tina Shields, has said the district will not raise. The Metropolitan Water District of Southern California, the region's largest urban importer, spends 25 cents an acre-foot for around 850,000 acre-feet of Colorado River water. The federal government supplies more than 7 million acre-feet across California, Arizona, and Nevada for less than $1 per acre-foot. The water in the river is, for the agencies that hold the paper, nearly free. What costs money is moving it, treating it, and buying it from someone who already holds it.

The Rio Grande shows the same spread at a different scale. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, in a 2026 study for the U.S. Department of Agriculture, valued irrigation water in the Lower Rio Grande Valley of Texas at $342.79 per acre-foot — the direct agricultural value of the water, in 2023 dollars — against $241 per acre-foot in Chihuahua and $194 in Tamaulipas across the border. The basin's treaty math explains why the number matters: the 2020–2025 cycle under the 1944 Water Treaty ended with Mexico delivering 884,861 acre-feet, only 50.6 percent of the 1.75 million acre-feet required, leaving a deficit of about 865,000 acre-feet, one of the largest shortfalls since 2002. In New Mexico, the New Mexico Water Rights Association's published figures put permanent rights at $35,000 to $45,000 per acre-foot per year in the Santa Fe area, $9,000 to $35,000 in the Middle Rio Grande, and $2,400 in the Roswell Artesian Basin — a spread driven by seniority, by proximity to a city that must buy, and by whether the aquifer underneath is still producing. Upstream in Colorado's San Luis Valley, the price is set by regulation rather than by trade: pumping fees that once stood at $75 an acre-foot reached $150, and the Rio Grande Water Conservation District's Subdistrict 1 has approved a rise to $500 an acre-foot under a plan to cut pumping and meet the Rio Grande Compact, with the new fees potentially starting in 2026 if they survive a court challenge, according to Big Pivots. Colorado lawmakers have already approved $30 million to help growers in the Republican River Basin cut use to meet that basin's compact.

Leases tell the story the permanent prices hide, because a lease is the price of one year of water rather than a century of it. In California's 2014 drought, spot prices reached $2,200 an acre-foot — ten times the level of five years earlier — as districts and landowners auctioned stored water to farmers keeping orchards alive. The Madera Irrigation District sold about 3,200 acre-feet for nearly $7 million that year, and the Del Puerto Water District paid an estimated $775 to $980 an acre-foot for groundwater pumped from neighboring Merced County. The gap between a $2,200 lease year and a $30 wholesale year is the gap between a paper right and wet water in a dry year. The same logic runs through the fallowing market: federal and district programs pay farmers about $400 an acre-foot to leave water in the river, according to an independent cost model published in October 2026, and moving that water 200 miles across the desert to a coastal city adds about $680 an acre-foot in conveyance and treatment, for a delivered cost of roughly $1,150. WestWater Research's annual market review counted 2.5 million acre-feet of western water deals worth a combined $1 billion in its latest report — a market big enough to be real and small enough that a single city's purchase, like Aurora's, moves the benchmark.

The counterargument is the one the C-BT price series makes against the scarcity story itself. Northern Water's unit price fell from its $67,000 peak in 2023 to $57,487 in 2025, and the per-acre-foot figure fell from $101,000 in 2022 to $85,000 in 2025 — during the two driest years in the modern record, with the worst April snowpack ever measured in 2026. If water prices tracked hydrology, those years should have set records. They did not, because C-BT buyers are not betting on the river. They are betting on houses: the price tracks Front Range development, and when housing starts slow, the price of water falls even as the reservoirs fall with it. The UCLA study makes the same point from the other side. The cheapest water in the West is not cheap because it is plentiful; it is cheap because a century-old contract says it is, and the most expensive water is not expensive because it is scarce; it is expensive because a city needs it delivered, treated, and reliable. Price is a function of paper, plumbing, and proximity. Hydrology is fourth.

That reframes the question a buyer should ask. Not what an acre-foot costs — the answer ranges from 12 cents to $85,000, and every figure in between is correct for someone — but what the acre-foot in question survives. A senior C-BT unit at $57,487 delivers 0.8 acre-feet in a drought year because the project is firmed by storage. An Imperial Valley acre-foot at $20 is cheap because the district's paper is senior and its farmers have no competing buyer. A Santa Fe acre-foot at $35,000 to $45,000 is expensive because the city must have it and the basin is closed. The spread between senior and junior rights is the market's own rating system, and it is more honest than any single price. The buyer who pays the benchmark price for a junior right has bought the number, not the water. The buyer who reads the spread — seniority, storage, delivery point, and the next shortage declaration — has bought the asset. In western water, the price is never the information. The spread is.
